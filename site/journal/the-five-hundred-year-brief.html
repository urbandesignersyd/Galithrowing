The Five-Hundred-Year Brief

Peter J Gartrell · October 2026

Most buildings are designed to a nominal life of fifty years. The figure is a convention of the building codes, but it has quietly hardened into an ambition. We design for the mortgage, the depreciation schedule and the planning cycle, then express surprise when the result is demolished within a single human lifetime.

Galithrowing begins from a different premise: every brief should be read as though it will be handed to a client in the year 2526.

I. The arithmetic of permanence

A structure that lasts five hundred years carries its embodied carbon across ten times the span of one that lasts fifty. The most sustainable building is rarely the one with the most efficient services. More often it is the one that people refuse to knock down because they love it, can repair it and can adapt it.

Longevity is not a material property. It is a cultural agreement renewed by every generation that chooses to keep something.

II. Designing for strangers

The five-hundred-year client is a stranger. We cannot know their technologies, their climate or their sense of beauty. What we can give them is legibility: structures whose logic can be read, whose joints can be opened and whose materials can be named and sourced again.

This is the spirit of the German Gesamtkunstwerk turned inside out. We are not making a total work of art that is closed and complete. We are making an open one, a framework that invites later hands to finish it.

III. Questions for every brief

Before we draw, we ask:

1. What will this place be asked to do once its first purpose has expired?
2. Which elements will need replacing, and has their replacement been drawn?
3. What will this site look like when the coastline, the canopy and the climate have shifted?
4. Would a stranger in five centuries recognise this as something worth keeping?

None of these questions has a final answer. Asking them, deliberately and early, is the discipline of the practice.
