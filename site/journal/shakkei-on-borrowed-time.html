Shakkei: On Borrowed Time

Peter J Gartrell · October 2026

In the Japanese garden tradition, shakkei (借景), meaning “borrowed scenery”, describes the incorporation of a distant mountain, a temple roof or a neighbour’s tree into the composition of a garden. The garden-maker owns none of it, yet frames all of it.

We think the idea carries further. Every act of building borrows more than the view. It borrows time: the time of the forest that became the timber, of the quarry that became the stone, and of the future occupants who will inherit whatever we leave behind.

The debt we do not record

Balance sheets record what a project costs today. They do not record the amenity it removes from the year 2200, or the maintenance it bequeaths to the year 2400. Shakkei asks us to acknowledge what lies beyond the boundary. A five-hundred-year practice asks us to acknowledge what lies beyond the lease.

Frame the distant mountain, and remember that you are someone else’s distant mountain.

Practising the borrowing

In practice this means drawing the views a building will give to its neighbours as carefully as the views it takes. It means leaving room for canopy that will not mature for a century, and accepting that the finest moment of a landscape may arrive long after its designer has gone.

That is not a loss. It is the condition of every garden worth making.
